\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:introduction}\comment{Added: chapter}
All robots suffer a certain degree of wear during their lifetime: unforeseen damages to structures, joints that become less (or more) frictional, motors and batteries that gradually lose power over time, etc.\ These changes result in evolving dynamics of the robot, which can be detrimental to the control of the robot.
Consider a simplified example where a robot arm needs to reach a certain position, whereas normally a torque $x$ applied for $y$ seconds will result in a perfect completion of the task, with a less powerful motor or a smoother joint, these controls will make the arm miss its target.

A solution to this could be that the user has instructions to update the software of the robot every few weeks; or preferably, the robot receives software updates which the user only needs to download and install; or even better, the robot automatically updates itself when necessary\dots\
Enter \emph{lifelong learning}, a concept that is generally understood to relate to systems that are able to adapt to changes they might encounter throughout their lifetime.

This literature study aims to provide an extensive overview of various techniques that can be employed to enable lifelong learning in robot control.
The study will provide essential background for the thesis, which will aim to achieve lifelong learning in the control of a small origami robot.

A clarification of the term `origami robot' might be useful first: an origami robot is not an origami-folding robot, but rather a robot constructed by origami techniques.
This includes designs made out of flexible materials such as thin plastics or paper, where the folds can provide both structural integrity and serve as actuation mechanisms.
These approaches bring with them advantages such as compact configurations and miniaturization, self-deployment, or unconventional actuation mechanisms (magnetic, thermal deformation,  \dots)~\cite{Tang2022a}.

Origami robots are a subset of soft robots, whose emergence over recent years has changed the robotics landscape.
Soft robots' flexibility and use of  non-rigid materials makes them inherently compliant and adaptive.
The use of soft materials comes with numerous advantages.\ Their compliance makes them a safer alternative in human-robot interaction settings than traditional robotics~\cite{Sun2021}, and their use of flexible materials allows for greater manoeuvrability and energy-efficient locomotion~\cite{Sun2021, Kim2021}.


\section{Application to soft (origami) robots}\label{sec:origami}
Why apply the lifelong learning setting to soft (origami) robots?\ To begin with, the flexibility of soft robots makes modeling them a challenging task, and control of these robots thus tends towards machine learning-based methods~\cite{Kim2021}.
Additionally, consider the illustrative example about the software updates for a conventional robot:
whereas manually updating a robot's control algorithm every few weeks might not be such expensive trade-off, updating a soft robot every few days or even hours is considerably more cumbersome.
Due to the materials they are constructed from, soft robots degrade a lot faster than conventional robots and, although they are more compliant and thus in some way more resistive to damages, their materials are less sturdy and more prone to tearing.
Therefore, lifelong adaptive techniques are a viable choice to ensure that a soft robot remains useful and operational throughout its lifetime.

Alternatively, deploying lifelong learning for conventional robots could be useful as well, but then evaluating whether a certain approach works could take several months or years.
Soft robots provide a good setting for testing various methods over a shorter period of time.


\section{Challenges}\label{sec:challenges}\
Successfully enabling lifelong learning faces a number of challenges.\ Some key challenges are addressed by \textcite{Lesort2020}, such as \emph{catastrophic forgetting}, \emph{handling memories}, and \emph{detecting distributional shifts}.

Catastrophic forgetting relates to robots forgetting previously learned knowledge as they progress through life.\ Preferably, a robot should be able to quickly learn to adapt to new settings, without thereby `overwriting' or otherwise erasing old knowledge.

Various ways of retaining old knowledge will be discussed, such as storing it somewhere for reusing it later.
Storing memories can be done in many ways, and should aim to be as memory-efficient as possible, allowing for fast searches and retrieval of knowledge; another key challenge of handling memories.

As the goal is for a robot to adapt to changes throughout its lifetime, it is essential that it can detect these changes.
This is related to detecting significant shifts in the data landscape it encounters.\ These shifts can be caused by imbalanced input data, a change in environment, the introduction of new tasks, or - the main focus of this study - the evolution of the robot's dynamics.
